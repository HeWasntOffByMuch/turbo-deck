Sixteen days after the world went 3D

Spec 031 stood the flat top-down arena up into a blocked, flat-shaded isometric scene. Everything since is downstream of that one decision, and it has not stopped compounding: 737 commits and 139 specs, of which 428 commits and 90 specs landed in the six days since this page was last written.

That stretch added three subsystems that did not exist in any previous roadmap — a GUI framework, a unit-authoring format, and a tab that spends real money — while seven of the twelve things I predicted did not move at all. The scorecard is below, and this time the misses say something different than they did last time.

Seven moments where the project stopped being what it was

Each of these is a deletion or a reversal as much as an addition, which remains the most distinctive thing about how this codebase moves.

Jul 31 · specs 043–044

The ground stopped being flat

A flat slab became layered heightfields, the arena walls came off, and trees were promoted from decoration to colliders. Every system written after this assumes a world, not a level.

flat slab → heightfield chunks

Aug 5 · spec 062

The deck was deleted

The card hand, the poker combos, the parry window and the adrenaline economy — the entire premise of the name — were removed in a single commit and replaced with wind-ups, projectiles and cooldowns.

−14,652 / +2,958 lines · 90 files

Aug 5 · spec 057

Two simulations became one

The client's simulation was not synchronised with the server's; it was deleted. src/sim/ shrank to geometry helpers, and the tick rate split from the broadcast rate — 60Hz for the game, 20Hz for the wire.

2 sims → 1 · 60 Hz / 20 Hz

Aug 6 · spec 072

The editor's world became the played world

The renderer stopped building a world from a seed. The world is baked to maps/arena.json, the server ticks that document, and the client asks for the ground underfoot. The editor's output is the game's input.

seed → document → wire → screen

Aug 9 · specs 108–113

The game started spending money

A Studio tab that generates units through a third-party model API. The spec is explicitly not "call the API" — it is the ledger, the cost projection shown before you confirm, the rig-check that aborts before a paid call, a per-day ceiling, and a cache so nobody buys the same model twice. It is also the first time the browser ever spoke HTTP to the game server.

Aug 10–11 · specs 123–138

The interface became a subsystem

A GUI framework written from scratch: layout, focus, drag, layers, motion, sound, a router and a window manager, with screens for inventory, shop, trade, character, keybindings and options. Pure and headless — "a gui the tests can see" — and checked against 32 golden images. The same architectural move as the sim, applied to the UI.

How the last roadmap scored

The previous revision made twelve calls. Ninety specs landed after it. One call landed outright, two landed halfway, one reversed, and seven did not move — while the bulk of the work went somewhere no row on the table mentioned.

3 landed

  • A real inventoryCompletely. Spec 126 gave ownership a place to live — before it, any client could equip anything in the table. Then bags, drag, a shop, currency, a character screen and a two-player trade.
  • Corpses, then lootHalf. Ownership, containers and a swap that moves four of them atomically all exist. Nothing drops into them: no lootTable, no ground item.
  • More weather, more worldSideways. Not rain or a second biome — instead a whole-renderer look pass (097–106) and verticality: rocks you can stand on, tiers, cliffs, stairs with real steps.

5 missed entirely

  • A GUI frameworksrc/ui/, 82 files, headless and golden-tested.
  • StudioA paid generation pipeline with a ledger, a ceiling and a cache.
  • Units as a formatsrc/units/, 36 files: a unit is three files, GLB parsing, mesh checks, bone naming, a state machine.
  • The phoneTouch controls, a HUD that fits, device detection, and a build that hides five of six tabs on a phone.
  • Effects and verticalityParticles as array rows, blood, decals, smoke, auras, explosions — and a world with tiers.

Last revision I concluded the misses were all taste — look and feel, things that leave no structural trace the way an unread interface does. That diagnosis was wrong. This time the misses are three subsystems with their own top-level directories, 200 files between them, and more tests than the whole project had a week ago. They are as structural as anything gets.

The real pattern is simpler and less flattering. An Out of scope section describes a deferral inside a thread that is already running. It is excellent at predicting the next spec in that thread and says nothing whatsoever about which entirely new thread opens next — and new threads are where most of the work goes. Seven of my twelve rows were deferrals, and all seven are still deferrals; ninety specs happened beside them. A roadmap read off interfaces predicts continuations, not decisions.

The revised roadmap

Same sourcing discipline, with one addition: rows carry how long they have been deferred. On the evidence above, age is the more informative column — a deferral that has survived two revisions is not queued, it is declined.

What this becomes

Not a game that ships. An engine and a toolchain of unusual quality, with a game-shaped hole where the thing you play is supposed to be — and the hole is now the most load-bearing feature of the codebase.

I have revised this section down twice and it needs revising down again. The architectural call was right and keeps being right: the editor authors the world, the server owns it, the client draws and predicts it, and content is data all the way through — a unit is three files, a species is a data file, an item is a row, an effect is a row. Sixteen days in, that structure has held under 158,000 lines without bending. It is genuinely impressive work.

But look at what the last six days actually bought. A GUI framework — for a game with one player. A trade table with two sides — for a game with one player. A paid pipeline for generating a roster — which contains one mannequin. An inventory with ownership enforcement — that nothing drops into. Every one of these is the tool for a thing that does not exist yet, built to a standard that assumes it will.

And the one instrument that measured the thing itself was deleted. That is the fact I would not have predicted and cannot argue away. This project instruments everything it can photograph — 26 preview rigs, 10 probes, 32 goldens, 143 screenshots — and removed the one harness pointed at whether the fight is any good. Two revisions ago I said nothing in the workflow would flag that. Nothing did; CLAUDE.md still advertises the harness that isn't there.

So the prediction is narrower and more specific than before. This becomes an engine other people build on — the map format, the unit format, the headless UI, the determinism lint and the preview rigs are all more reusable than the arena they were built for, and the whole thing runs and is tested without a screen, which is rare and valuable. The game on top will keep being the least finished part, not through neglect but because every path out of a hard content question leads to a well-specified system instead, and the workflow rewards that turn every time. The clearest signal to watch is a trivial one: the first commit that adds a second player, or the first that makes a monster drop something. Either would mean the project has decided to be played rather than built.
