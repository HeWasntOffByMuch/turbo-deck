Five moments where the project stopped being what it was

Each of these is a deletion as much as an addition, which remains the most distinctive thing about how this codebase moves.

Jul 31 · specs 043–044

The ground stopped being flat

A flat slab became layered heightfields, the arena walls came off, and trees were promoted from decoration to colliders. Every system written after this assumes a world, not a level — which is why the pathfinder was still being taught new things about gaps a week later.

flat slab → heightfield chunks + scatter

Aug 5 · spec 062

The deck was deleted

The card hand, the poker combos, the parry window and the adrenaline economy — specs 002 through 030, the entire premise of the name — were removed in a single commit and replaced with wind-ups, projectiles, cooldowns and a cast you can withdraw from.

−14,652 / +2,958 lines · 90 files

Aug 5 · spec 057

Two simulations became one

The client's simulation was not synchronised with the server's; it was deleted. src/sim/ shrank to geometry and collision helpers, the server's tick became the only authority, and the tick rate split from the broadcast rate — 60Hz for the game, 20Hz for the wire.

2 sims → 1 · 60 Hz sim / 20 Hz wire

Aug 5–6 · specs 063, 066, 069

The client stopped being a viewer

The Play tab was repointed at GameClient.view(), the 2D Pixi renderer was removed outright, and the client learned to predict — first the walk, then the blow, committing on screen the moment you ask rather than when it is told.

pixi.js: still a dependency, zero imports

Aug 6 · spec 072

The editor's world became the played world

The renderer stopped building a world from a seed. The generated world is baked to maps/arena.json, the server ticks that document, and the client holds the chunks that arrived while asking for the ground underfoot. The editor's output is now the game's input.

seed → document → wire → screen

The misses share a shape. Every one of them is about how the world looks or how the mouse feels, and none of them leaves a structural trace in the tree the way a ZoneManager or an unread DataStore interface does. The previous roadmap was derived from interfaces and out-of-scope notes, so it could only ever see the thread that writes them down. A third of this project's output is taste, and taste does not declare itself in the type system.

What this becomes

A small, self-hostable, deterministic multiplayer action-RPG world — authored in its own editor, owned by its own server, and playable inside a retro isometric renderer that is deliberately not allowed to know any rules.

That call stands, and the last day strengthened it. The editor's output is now the server's input and the client's stream; a marker in a map file spawns a monster in a running game. Those were three separate systems a week ago and they are one pipeline now. What remains between here and a genuinely multiplayer session is collider paging and a URL — one real spec and one line.

The medium term is content, and this is where I was too pessimistic last time. I argued that everything so far had been mechanism — the thing this workflow is extraordinarily good at — and that taste could not be regression-tested. The weather work answers that: there is now a preview rig that drives the real page, photographs it, and keeps 46 screenshots in the repository, and it found a shader bug on the way. A flat-leaved tree was built, photographed, judged against the light, and deleted. That is taste running through exactly the same loop as mechanism — build it, look at it, keep the record, remove what fails.

So the revised risk is narrower and more specific. Look and feel have a harness; combat balance still does not, and it is the one thing on the roadmap that no screenshot and no determinism test can settle. Two specs in a row have now declined to move a single windupTicks. The project can keep adding systems indefinitely without ever confronting whether the fight is fun, and nothing in the workflow will flag it.

The likeliest outcome is still an unusually well-engineered sandbox whose architecture gets linked around more than its gameplay does — but the gap is closing faster than I credited. The likeliest surprise is unchanged: that the headless, deterministic, agent-verifiable core turns out to be the actual product, and the game is what it was built to prove.
