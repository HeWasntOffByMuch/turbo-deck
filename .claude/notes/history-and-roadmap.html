Four moments where the project stopped being what it was

Each of these is a deletion as much as an addition, which is the most distinctive thing about how this codebase moves.

Jul 31 · specs 043–044

The ground stopped being flat

A flat slab became layered heightfields, the arena walls came off, and trees were promoted from decoration to colliders. Every system written after this assumes a world, not a level — which is why the pathfinder is still being taught new things about gaps a week later.

flat slab → heightfield chunks + scatter

Aug 5 · spec 062

The deck was deleted

The card hand, the poker combos, the parry window and the adrenaline economy — specs 002 through 030, the entire premise of the name — were removed in a single commit and replaced with wind-ups, projectiles, cooldowns and a cast you can withdraw from.

−14,652 / +2,958 lines · 90 files

Aug 5 · spec 057

Two simulations became one

The client's simulation was not synchronised with the server's; it was deleted. src/sim/ shrank to geometry and collision helpers, the server's tick became the only authority, and the tick rate split from the broadcast rate — 60Hz for the game, 20Hz for the wire.

2 sims → 1 · 60 Hz sim / 20 Hz wire

Aug 5–6 · specs 063, 066, 067

The client stopped being a viewer

The Play tab was repointed at GameClient.view(), the 2D Pixi renderer was removed outright, and the client learned to predict — committing to a blow the moment you ask rather than when it is told, and easing corrections instead of snapping.

pixi.js: still a dependency, zero imports

What this becomes

A small, self-hostable, deterministic multiplayer action-RPG world — authored in its own editor, owned by its own server, and playable inside a retro isometric renderer that is deliberately not allowed to know any rules.

The roguelite framing is already gone and is not coming back. A roguelite does not need a zone manager, a network chunk grid separate from the meshing one, a swappable DataStore, a token-gated admin namespace with an audit log, or a wire protocol that counts bytes. Those are all in the tree now, and every one of them is load-bearing only for a world that persists and holds more than one person.

The near term is convergence rather than expansion: the editor's output becoming the world you actually play, prediction extending from movement to the cast, and ws:// getting wired to the tab. Each of those is a join between two systems that already exist — which is why they will land fast. Expect the first genuinely multiplayer session within a handful of days of work, not weeks.

The medium term is content, and this is where the shape of the risk changes. Everything built so far has been mechanism, and mechanism is what this workflow is extraordinarily good at: 1,046 tests, a linter that mechanically enforces the determinism rules, a headless harness that can screenshot the real rig without a screen. Loot tables, encounter design and balance are not mechanism, they are taste, and they cannot be regression-tested. The spec-first habit will keep producing correct systems; whether it produces a game anyone wants to play for an hour is the open question the codebase has so far been able to defer.

The likeliest ceiling, honestly: an unusually well-engineered, unusually well-documented sandbox — the kind of project whose architecture gets linked around more than its gameplay does. The likeliest surprise: that the headless, deterministic, agent-verifiable core turns out to be the actual product, and the game is what it was built to prove.
