A month after the world went 3D

Spec 031 stood the flat top-down arena up into a blocked, flat-shaded isometric scene. One month later: 1,467 commits, 267 specs, 301,290 lines and 7,410 tests. Multiplayer, accounts, a database, loot, an economy, six progression tracks, afflictions, sound, a village with shopkeepers who are people.

The last revision of this page called it "an engine and a toolchain with a game-shaped hole where the thing you play is supposed to be." That was wrong, and it was wrong fast. Seven of its twelve predictions landed, including both of the falsifiable signals it named as the test of whether this project had decided to be played rather than built.

Last revision I concluded that a deferral surviving two revisions is "not queued, it is declined", and put an age column on the roadmap to make the point. That was also wrong. Every row I marked six days stale landed within a week of my marking it — collider paging, a second player, loot, aggro, persistence. Age predicted nothing.

What actually happens is that this project runs a thread until it is out of obvious next steps, then turns and clears a batch of deferrals from a different one. Deferrals are not declined and they are not queued: they are batched, and the batch is picked when a thread exhausts itself. That explains both the seven that landed and the four that did not — the four are all still inside threads with obvious next steps of their own, so nothing has needed to turn to them yet.

The revised roadmap

The age column is gone; it measured nothing. What is left is the systems gap — genuinely short now — and a new category that has been growing quietly the whole time: everything between a finished game and a person playing it.

What this becomes

A finished game nobody can reach. The systems argument is over and the project won it — what is left between turbo-deck and players is a URL, a front door, and somebody being told which button attacks.

I have now been wrong about this project in two opposite directions. Revision 1 said it would become a small multiplayer action-RPG, which was right. Revision 3 said it never would — that every subsystem was "the tool for a thing that does not exist yet", and listed seven of them as evidence. Six of those seven resolved within a fortnight. The two-player trade table got two players. The inventory got drops. The memory store became a database. The one-arena chunk manager got a map of many files, a shore and a village. The cosmetic VFX library got an aura that damages you. The absent fun-harness came back.

I also named the falsifiable test myself — "the first commit that adds a second player, or the first that makes a monster drop something" — and said either would mean the project had decided to be played rather than built. Both happened, thirteen days apart. I should have weighted the evidence in front of me over the pattern I had fitted to it: a project that builds the tool before the thing is not avoiding the thing, it is just building in that order.

So the prediction is now about the only gap left, and it is not a systems gap. This is a game with lag compensation and no title screen. It has accounts, sessions and a per-connection hardening pass, and it calls you Wanderer. It deploys automatically on every push to a static host that cannot run its own server. A stranger opening the public build meets seven tabs, six of which are developer tools, and no indication of which one is the game.

That gap will close too — the pattern is now well established enough that predicting otherwise would be the same mistake a third time. What I would watch instead is what happens after the door opens, because it is the first question this workflow has faced that no test, probe, golden image or balance harness can answer: whether people stay. Everything up to here has been a problem the repository could pose to itself. That one has to come from outside it.
